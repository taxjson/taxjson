taxjson.com1

The problem

The slip your broker files is not your cost basis

A T5008 reports proceeds per account. The Income Tax Act taxes you on one average cost per security across all your accounts, denies losses when any affiliated account — your RRSP included — bought the same thing within 30 days, and folds option premiums, splits, spin-offs and return of capital into that cost. Nobody's statement does that math for you.

Cross-account pooling

s.47: one ACB pool per identical property. A US listing and its TSX listing are the same pool; a CDR is not.

Superficial losses

s.54 / s.40(2)(g): a loss is denied if you or an affiliated person bought within ±30 days and still holds. Denied into an RRSP, it is gone for good.

Everything that moves cost

Assignments (IT-479R), splits, spin-offs (s.86.1), mergers with elections, ROC, reinvested distributions — in the right order, on the settlement date.

Spreadsheets get the easy 90% right. The other 10% is where reassessments come from.
taxjson.com2

What it does

Three steps, all on disk

inputs/Broker CSV exports per account: IBKR, Questrade, RBC, Webull, Kraken, Coinbase — plus plain-text .tt for anything else
→
taxjson runParse · convert at the Bank of Canada rate per trade date · pool identical securities · corporate actions with elections · superficial-loss pass across taxable and registered accounts
→
reports/Per-account summaries, the wash radar, harvest candidates, T1135, a tax estimate, Schedule 3 / Form 8949 rows — and the broker cross-check

One config file

taxjson.toml: year, country, province, base currency, one section per account with its type (taxable / sheltered) and the broker positions files to check against.

Row accounting

Every parser accounts for every row it reads. Rows it recognises but does not translate are counted and named — never silently dropped.

Traceable numbers

taxjson audit recomputes every disposition from the parsed broker row through FX, ACB and the wash determination, and ties it out against the reports.

Worked example

A loss you cannot claim

What happened

Margin account buys 200 shares in January, sells 400 at a loss on February 3. The RRSP has held 800 of the same stock since the previous year.

What the slip says

A capital loss on 400 shares. The spreadsheet agrees.

What the Act says

200 shares were acquired inside the window and the affiliated RRSP still owns the stock at its end: the loss on those 200 is superficial. The ACB bump goes to the RRSP — where it is worthless. Permanently denied.

NOTE: superficial loss — 200 sh denied (permanently: replacement property held in rrsp)
  loss sale 2026-02-03 · trigger lots 2026-01-08, 2026-01-13 · window end 2026-03-05
Only a tool that sees every account at once can get this right. That is the whole design.

Verification

Checked against the one thing that cannot lie

Internal reports can all agree and still be wrong. taxjson sanity compares the positions the books say you hold with the positions your broker's own export says you hold, account by account. Any difference is a trade that has not reached the CSV yet, or a booking problem — there is no third kind.

2,100+ tests

Every fix lands with a regression test that failed before it. No mocks for tax math.

Three property fuzzers

Conservation of gains under the wash rule, custody-transfer netting, split-during-settlement books — thousands of random ledgers per run.

Eight audit rounds

Adversarial reviews, statutory scenarios by hand, parser coverage on real exports, a pre-release security audit. Every finding reproduced, fixed, pinned.

Published limits

KNOWN_ISSUES.md lists what is out of scope and what to do by hand, with the citation.

Privacy and security

Nothing personal leaves the machine

Local by construction

Parsing, pooling and the wash pass run on your computer. The only network calls are FX rates (Bank of Canada) and crypto prices for unpriced rows — both switchable off with TAXJSON_OFFLINE=1. SECURITY.md lists every egress.

No credentials stored

Broker fetch uses your own token file; it is never written into a project or sent anywhere but that broker.

The project guards itself

A personal-data scan runs in every gate and as the pre-push hook, fails closed, and reads a private denylist kept outside the repository. Redacted exports keep every row shape and none of the identifiers.

Zero runtime dependencies in the core. A tax tool has to be trustworthy before it is clever.

Scope

Canada is the product. The US engine is a draft.

Canada — supported

Average-cost ACB, superficial-loss rule across accounts, settlement-date timing, options, corporate actions with elections, T1135, Schedule 3, instalments and a marginal-rate estimate with provincial tables. Validated against two years of real multi-account books and the brokers' positions.

US — experimental

FIFO, §1091 wash sales, short/long-term, Form 8949 codes and a TXF export. Implemented and unit-tested, never validated on a real account. It says so at every run. Real US exports — redacted — are the way to change that.

Honest labelling costs nothing. A tax tool that overclaims loses the only thing it has.

Install

Your first books in ten minutes

bash -c "$(curl -fsSL https://taxjson.com/install.sh)"

Installs the latest release into its own Python environment and links taxjson onto your PATH. Needs git and Python 3.9+, nothing else. Re-run to upgrade.

mkdir -p ~/taxes/2026 && cd ~/taxes/2026
taxjson init --country canada     # writes taxjson.toml and inputs/
# drop broker CSVs into inputs/margin/, inputs/rrsp/, …
taxjson run                       # books, reports, wash pass, broker check
taxjson sum                       # the realized-gains summary
taxjson wash-radar                # "can I sell this at a loss today?"
